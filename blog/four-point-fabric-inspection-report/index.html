How to Read a Four-Point Fabric Inspection Report

An inspection report puts one number at the top, a figure such as 28.6 points per 100 square yards quoted without its formula, and a decision follows: accept the roll, claim against it, or send it back. The number looks measured. It is a division, and behind it sit conventions buyer and seller may have set differently: what was counted, what area it was divided by, and which rolls the answer describes.

Key takeaways

  • ›A four-point score is total penalty divided by area, so the denominator can decide the verdict as surely as the faults do6.
  • ›One hypothetical roll with 35 points reads 18.10 per 100 yd² and 21.65 per 100 m², a step of 19.6% that comes from the unit, not the cloth (Hossain and Islam, 2023)67.
  • ›Whether a small fault scores at all is a contract choice: one published convention excludes minor faults, another scores every fault by size76.
  • ›A score per roll and an average per shipment answer different questions, and either may be used for acceptance by agreement1.
  • ›No standard sets a pass mark. ASTM's own text says the same rolls can score very differently under different options1.

What the four-point score actually measures

A four-point score counts penalised faults and divides them by the area inspected. A penalty point is the unit being counted, assigned by a size band6 or by an expert's judgement of kind, severity and amount7. Tastimur, Akin and Agrikli call the four-point system the method used to regulate fabric quality in the apparel sector, and note that the Dallas system, the Graniteville 78 method, the four-point system and the ten-point system all remain in use7. A report that does not name its system has given a number without a unit of meaning.

The bands come first. Hossain and Islam print them in their Table 1, recorded as following ASTM D5430-13(2017): a fault up to 3 in scores 1, over 3 in and up to 6 in scores 2, over 6 in and up to 9 in scores 3, over 9 in scores 4, and any hole except a pin hole scores 4 whatever its size6. Size is not the only axis either: Tastimur and colleagues describe criteria an expert sets on the kind, severity and amount of a fault, with points from one to four7. The two can score one fault differently, so the report has to say which it used. Hossain and Islam's study is knit work from Bangladeshi circular knitting factories, so its bands describe that setting rather than woven silk.

The standard behind the looking is not a rule for accepting. ASTM D5430 describes procedures to examine and grade fabrics by visual inspection, and states that when purchaser and seller agree, those procedures may be used for acceptance of individual rolls and shipments1. The permission is conditional. What the score then does not hold matters as much: it records neither where the faults sat, nor what they were, nor which process made them, and two rolls can share a score while being unsellable in different places.

The denominator is where reports go wrong

The formula Hossain and Islam use is points per 100 yd² equals total points multiplied by 36 and by 100, divided by the product of roll length in yards and cuttable width in inches6. The constants are geometry. Area in square inches is length in yards times 36 times width in inches, and 100 yd² is 129,600 in², so 129,600 ÷ 36 gives 3,600; in metric, 100 m² is 1,000,000 cm², and 1,000,000 ÷ 100 gives 10,000. A report that prints its formula lets a reader check both halves.

Cuttable width is the width of cloth a cutting table can use, and it is measured in a tension-free relaxed state4. It is not the width the loom was set to. ISO 22198 sets out that determination of length and width, covering full width, folded and tubular fabric up to 100 m, and states that it does not treat construction defects or other defects4. Width and faults on one sheet come from two measurements.

The numerator carries a convention too. Tastimur and colleagues state that minor faults do not contribute to the penalty points, and that fabric quality is graded in points per 100 m²7. The band table works the other way, since a fault of 3 in or less still carries a point6. Both are published procedures and neither is right in the abstract; the choice belongs in the contract and on every report, since a report that counts minor faults and one that ignores them are not comparable.

Row b is the quiet one. A loom width of 60 in spreads the same penalty over more area and lowers the reading. ASTM anticipates this: the penalty points obtained in grading the same rolls may vary considerably under each of the options listed, and the same option should be used when purchaser and supplier disagree1.

Row c is the loud one. Under the square-metre convention Tastimur and colleagues use7, the same 35 points become 21.65 per 100 m², a ratio of 1.196, and the nineteen percent is not a change in the cloth: one square yard is 0.83612736 m², so points per square yard must grow by the reciprocal of that figure when counted per square metre.

Row e costs money. Count the four faults the square-metre convention would ignore at one point each, and 35 becomes 39, so the formula returns 20.17 per 100 yd². Against a contract limit of 20 the roll passes at 18.10, fails at 20.17, and fails again if 21.65 is read against 20 without noticing the unit. One roll, one set of faults, three verdicts.

No measurement was taken for this table; the method is arithmetic on the two published formulas, and the methodology was checked by recomputing each output from its inputs before publication.

One roll is not the lot

ASTM D5430 permits acceptance of individual rolls and of shipments in one clause, subject to agreement1. Those are two populations, and a report that does not say which it scored cannot settle a dispute about the other. A shipment average is built from roll scores, so a lot can sit inside a limit while one roll sits outside it, and the average cannot name that roll. A per-roll score says nothing about the next roll. Published lot-level work shows the scale involved: Hossain and Islam report 55,524.91 m² inspected, made up of 18,405.10 m² of single jersey, 27,308.67 m² of fleece and 9,811.14 m² of 1X1 rib6.

For example, a constructed lot of five rolls might average 18 points per 100 yd² while one carries 31, and no lot-level total lets a buyer find that roll. The figures are invented, not measured.

The unit of sale matters too. One peer listing for a slubbed silk sells by the roll, with a minimum of one roll at roughly 30 to 35 m beside composition, widths, weight band, texture, drape, care and customisation as separate fields12. Beneath it sits a human layer: published work records that fault detection in the knitting industry is done manually by the human naked eye8.

The most frequent defect is not the most costly one

Hossain and Islam put the problem in a sentence: the subjective nature of defect detection by knitters and inspectors often causes wrong emphasis on frequently occurring defects instead of on influential ones6. In their data gout on 1X1 rib was judged both the most frequent and the most influential, and the paper treats that as a misjudgement6.

The mechanism belongs to counting. A defect list sorted by occurrence makes its first line the headline, but that column counts events; the point column weights them, by size6 or by kind, severity and amount7. A report can be accurate and still mislead a reader who treats count as cost.

The categories give the count its use. ISO 8498 defines woven-fabric defects as characteristics unintentionally introduced into the fabric, and sorts them into general, yarn, weft, warp, selvedge, and faults that appear only after finishing3. A count by name hides which of those owns the fault; a count by category points at a machine, a yarn lot or a finishing bath, where the standard also says the presence of a listed characteristic does not automatically imply that the fabric is sub-standard3.

When a slub is not a defect

Some thickness variation exists before the cloth is woven. CAMEO describes dupion silk as an irregular thread produced when threads from two or more cocoons are spun together, giving a rough yarn with uneven thick and thin areas9. The London Museum's doppiona record adds the consequence: the thicker thread contains irregularities, but the silk woven from it dyes well and creases less10.

When a report lists slubs or thick places, the question is not whether they exist. ISO 8498's definition turns on whether a characteristic was unintentionally introduced3, and in this yarn the variation enters when filaments are combined910. The question is whether that size and frequency were accepted when the cloth was specified.

Suppliers answer it at product level. One product page carries a disclaimer covering shade variation and natural imperfections, advises allowing extra material, and offers a sample before an order11; another lists texture, drape, care and customisation as separate fields12. Both put the boundary in the specification, ahead of any roll being examined.

A score is not an AQL

The two terms get used as if exchangeable. Acceptance quality limit is the maximum average percentage of defective items considered acceptable during random sampling, and ISO 2859-1 names it the AQL2. Its unit is a percentage of units, while a four-point score's unit is points per area67. One is a proportion of failing rolls, the other a density of penalty.

The 2026 edition is the third. It adds skip-lot sampling procedures and replaces the edition of 1999, and it carries switching rules under which worsening quality moves a supplier to tightened inspection and improving quality to reduced inspection2.

The two can disagree without either being wrong: a lot can pass an attribute sampling plan and still carry a high penalty density, and a lot can fail on one defective roll while the average score looks comfortable. For example, a shipment can clear a sampling plan because few rolls fail the pass or fail test while those same rolls carry a penalty density above the agreed limit, since the plan never weighs area.

The published summary of ISO 2859-1 sets out no acceptance and rejection figures, so those must come from the standard2. Pass marks sit in the same place: neither ASTM D5430 nor ISO 8498 states a score at which cloth becomes unacceptable13. Acceptable points per 100 yd² mostly depend on buyer and seller agreeing, and the 20 and 30 point bands are the study's own6.

Automation changes none of this. Machine vision has been shown to perform four-point scoring on a benchmark set of seven defect classes recorded on greige cloth and on cloth after wet processing8. That changes who finds the faults, not what they mean.

What to write into the inspection clause

A short list turns a report from an announcement into a document a buyer can act on.

1. The system, named, with its option set: four-point, and then whether faults are banded by size6 or judged on kind, severity and amount7.

2. The minor fault rule, since one convention excludes minor faults and another scores a small fault76.

3. The width, defined as cuttable width and measured in a tension-free relaxed state4, with a line stating that loom width is not the denominator.

4. The denominator and its constant, printed as a formula: 3,600 for points per 100 yd²6, 10,000 for points per 100 m²7.

5. The population and sampling plan, per roll or per shipment or by a named sampling standard and edition2, with score and pass or fail kept apart.

6. The defect vocabulary by category rather than by name, so counts can be compared between reports3.

7. The inherent variation accepted for the cloth ordered, as a slub size, a frequency per unit of length and a statement of where in the piece it is counted, taken from the product specification, not the inspector's judgement91011.

8. The tie-break: where buyer and supplier disagree, both grade with the same point assignment option1.

9. Length and width per roll, since a score without its inputs cannot be rechecked, and, where weight is specified, which weight, because mass per unit length and mass per unit area diverge as structure varies5.

None of this sets a pass mark, which is the point. A score just inside a limit is not good cloth and one just outside is not bad cloth; the number acquires meaning only against a threshold two parties signed, computed by a formula both can reproduce, over a population both had in mind. Where the cloth is a slubbed silk, the specification must also say which thick places were bought on purpose910. The production-side case is set out in how to agree a slub tolerance in the contract. Every claim on this page was checked against the source cited for it before publication.
