Measurement / testing2026-11-16

How Silk Fabric Weight Is Measured, and Why a Swatch Is Not a Lot

A mass per unit area result is a division: grams of cloth over square metres of cloth. The division is the easy part. What decides whether the number describes your shipment is what went on the balance, how many pieces of it, and in what air. ASTM D3776 leaves those choices to four named options2(https://dlvn.khoweb24h.com/uploads/files/ASTM%20D3776-09.pdf), and its small-swatch option carries a sentence that buyers read past: the results are considered applicable to the sample only and not necessarily to the lot2. Two honest reports can still disagree about the same cloth.

Key takeaways

  • ›A g/m² figure is a mass over an area, so the population the mass came from decides what it describes2.
  • ›ASTM D3776 names its full-piece option for acceptance testing of commercial shipments and limits the small-swatch option to the sample2.
  • ›Conditioning moves the reading by an amount comparable to the tolerance a contract may set, so the air belongs in the report10.
  • ›A report without the option, the specimen count, the atmosphere and the balance tolerance cannot be checked against your own re-test29.
  • ›Width matters twice: once when a figure is converted, and once when the cloth is measured slack rather than under tension6(https://www.iso.org/standard/40768.html).

What the number on a spec sheet actually measures

Mass per unit area is a mass divided by an area1(https://www.iso.org/standard/9335.html), which is why the unit is written as grams over square metres rather than as a single weight. It is not thickness. ISO 5084 describes a method for the determination of the thickness of textiles and textile products under specified pressure, and it is not applicable to textile floor coverings, nonwovens, geotextiles and coated fabrics11(https://www.iso.org/standard/23348.html). It is not momme either, though momme is the trade's name for the same quantity; the conversion between the two units is set out in the momme guide.

The unit also tells you which quantity you hold. ISO 3801 gives the reason grams per metre and grams per square metre are not interchangeable by width alone. For some fabrics the two are related simply by the width, but for others variations in structure may introduce an important distinction between them1. Take a figured jacquard, for example, where the ground and the figure take up yarn at different rates. No single width converts one figure into the other.

Its scope is wider than buyers expect: woven fabrics including stretch types, made up full width or folded down the middle, complete pieces as well as sample lengths1. The folded state is the part that trips a conversion. Cloth presented folded down the middle and the same cloth opened out are two different widths, so a mass reported against length becomes a mass per unit area only once the width and the presentation are both known.

Four sampling options, and only one of them is a lot result

Option A is the full-piece route, the one ASTM D3776 ties to trade practice2(https://dlvn.khoweb24h.com/uploads/files/ASTM%20D3776-09.pdf). Its text says the option may be used for acceptance testing of commercial shipments since it has been used extensively in the trade. Option C is the small-swatch route, and it runs the other way: the results are considered to be applicable to the sample only and not necessarily to the lot2.

Table 1. The four sampling options named in ASTM D3776, as stated in the section text read for this page. "Not stated" marks cells the text does not cover.

Option B is named for the full width and Option D for narrow fabrics. A figure in grams per square metre alone has not said which of the four produced it2.

The small-sample method is also a standard in its own right. EN 12127 describes methods for mass per unit area using small samples in the standard atmosphere and/or oven-dry, for woven and knitted fabrics, and its contents include a sampling clause and an annex of suggested sampling5(https://standards.iteh.ai/catalog/standards/cen/6b6e76dd-ddd8-4271-84a1-d3f834f01b1d/en-12127-1997). Sample size is therefore a decision the method addresses rather than a detail left to an operator.

National systems adopt rather than copy. GB/T 4669-2008 is recorded as a modified adoption of ISO 3801:1977, covers whole pieces or one piece with elastic fabrics included, and references a standard atmosphere standard, an official moisture regain standard and a rounding rule7(https://openstd.samr.gov.cn/bzgk/std/newGbInfo?hcno=531DE82A840614255846157F4369461C). A national edition such as this one names what a mass result depends on: the air, the regain convention and the digits kept.

What the air in the room does to the reading

Conditioning is the exposure of a specimen to a stated atmosphere until its mass stops drifting4(https://www.astm.org/d1776_d1776m-20.html). ASTM D1776 makes the reason explicit. Because prior exposure of textiles to high or low humidity may affect moisture pick-up equilibrium, a preconditioning procedure is also given when required.

The atmosphere has numbers attached. ASTM D3776 asks for 21 ± 1 °C and 65 ± 2 % RH when textile specimens are weighed2. ISO 139 defines the standard atmosphere and a standard alternative that the parties may agree to use3(https://www.iso.org/standard/35179.html). The numeric detail behind that definition comes from the standard's catalogue listing, which transcribes a primary atmosphere of 20.0 °C and 65.0 % RH with tolerances of ±2.0 °C and ±4.0 % RH12(https://standards.iteh.ai/catalog/standards/sist/bc3ba05c-b1bf-4e50-9196-cca8b5bfe180/sist-en-iso-139-2005). The same listing transcribes 23.0 °C and 50.0 % RH for the alternative, used by agreement between the parties. It also gives a pre-conditioning band of 10 to 25 % RH under a ceiling of 50 °C, and an equilibrium criterion based on successive weighings at two-hour intervals. It adds that the run ends when no progressive mass change above 0.25 % remains, and that the test report must identify the specimen, cite ISO 139:2005 and state any deviations12.

The escape clause matters as much. ASTM D3776 states that when full rolls cannot be properly conditioned in reasonable time, tests are run without conditioning and the actual conditions are reported. It adds that such results may not correspond with results from adequately conditioned specimens2. An unconditioned report can be compliant and still not comparable.

Silk makes the difference visible. Nabi, Mohsin and Shah measured regain on Pakistani silk in 1972, using a vacuum desiccator and saturated salt solutions, at least four days at each step and a cycle of about five months10. Their setting was specific: 25 °C rather than 20 °C, on 1 g specimens. Nothing below is a constant for silk in general.

The same cloth weighed at 42.8% and 65.0% RH differs by 2.60% at one end of the paper's range and 3.33% at the other, while the two approach directions differ by 1.06% and 1.15%10(https://pjsir.org/multidisciplinary-archive/Volume%2015%201972/Issue%206/Article%2021%20Vol%2015%20Issue%206%201972.pdf). The mechanism behind both effects is recorded in ASTM D17764(https://www.astm.org/d1776_d1776m-20.html), which warns that prior exposure of textiles to high or low humidity may affect moisture pick-up equilibrium.

Table 2. Four recomputations from Nabi, Mohsin and Shah (1972), Table 3 and Table 5: arithmetic on published regain values, computed for this page and not measured here. Rows a and b step one cloth between two humidities; rows c and d compare the two directions at one humidity.

Rows a and b are a humidity step: one cloth, two humidities, weighed once at each. The spread between the lowest pair and the highest pair in the paper is also the spread between those two rows. Rows c and d are hysteresis, meaning cloth that arrives at one humidity from a drier or wetter state holds a different amount of water. Against a contract that allows three per cent either side, the humidity step alone reaches or passes the whole band before anyone questions the weaving10.

The paper also shows why regain is a convention rather than a constant. For its reference atmosphere at 25 °C, the authors suggest 11.0 as the official regain, and 10.5 for the practical conditions prevailing in Pakistan. They also find yellow above white and raw above degummed, because sericin takes up more moisture10. None of those values is a figure for Chinese silk at 20 °C, which is why the boundary sentence above matters.

China's standard for official moisture regain is GB/T 9994-2018, replacing the 2008 edition8(https://openstd.samr.gov.cn/bzgk/std/newGbInfo?hcno=BDBFD42F23E5CDEFF7CACC8F73FB3EA4&refer=outter). Its table values were not available here, so none are quoted.

The arithmetic, and the case where it fails

Once a specimen is on the balance the calculation is short: a square decimetre is 0.01 m², so the mass in grams divided by 0.01 is the mass multiplied by 100. The equipment sets the resolution. ASTM D3776 specifies a balance that weighs within ±0.1% of the mass of the specimens being tested, and a cutting die, square or round, of at least 13 cm²2(https://dlvn.khoweb24h.com/uploads/files/ASTM%20D3776-09.pdf). A 10 cm square die clears that floor at 100 cm², which is why the die size is worth asking about.

A result is usually an average. RISE, an ISO/IEC 17025 accredited laboratory, describes one test under the small-sample method as five specimens of 1 dm² each, conditioned in a defined climate and weighed9(https://www.ri.se/en/what-we-do/services/determination-of-mass-per-unit-area-using-small-samples). That description belongs to the laboratory and to the method it names, not to a clause of ISO 3801. It is also why a report showing one figure is hiding four others.

The method fails in identifiable places. Structure is the first: where it varies, mass per unit length and mass per unit area stop being convertible by width, the distinction ISO 3801 raises at the top of its scope1. Width is the second. ISO 22198:2006 specifies a method for the determination of length and width of textile fabrics that are in a tension-free relaxed state, for full-width, folded and tubular pieces no longer than 100 m6(https://www.iso.org/standard/40768.html). It also states that it does not specify a method to determine or describe construction defects or other defects. A width taken under tension, converted into a denominator, carries that tension into the answer.

ISO 3801 brings stretch fabrics inside its scope rather than leaving them out, so the same care applies to them1. Cut a specimen with a die smaller than the one specified, for instance, and the arithmetic stays right while the result gets less representative of the piece.

What a usable test report has to contain

A test report is usable against a re-test only when it names six things, and each of them maps to a clause above.

1. The option used, by letter or national equivalent, because a full piece and a swatch answer different questions27.

2. Specimen count and area, because one figure may be an average of five and the die area sets the arithmetic92.

3. Conditioning state and atmosphere, or the actual conditions if the test ran without conditioning243.

4. Balance tolerance, since a balance outside the tolerance the method sets cannot support the figure2.

5. Individual specimen results, not only the mean, because the spread between five specimens is information the mean removes9.

6. The standard and its edition, because clause numbering and detail differ between editions21.

The edition field is not pedantry. The copy of ASTM D3776 read for this page is the 09a print, reapproved in 2013; the current designation is D3776/D3776M-20, and the clause numbers above follow the text that was read2. A citation without an edition leaves the reader unable to tell which clause set the sampling.

Checking a supplier's figure against your own re-test

Order of questions decides the answer. Ask which option produced the figure, then how many specimens and at what area, then the conditioning state, then the balance tolerance. Only after those four does the number mean anything. A swatch result and a full-piece result answer for two populations, and the standard says so in one sentence for Option C2.

Then compare with a budget in mind. On silk the humidity step in Table 2 is worth more than a percentage point of the reading, and hysteresis at a steady humidity adds a second slice10. A re-test landing below a supplier's figure is not automatically a lighter cloth. It may be the same cloth in different air, so condition both and weigh both at one stated atmosphere24.

Do the re-test on a specimen large enough for the die and heavy enough for the balance, keep each result, and keep the atmosphere on the sheet29. Ask the person who signed the report, not a sales contact, for the individual readings. A kitchen scale and a squared-off offcut satisfy neither requirement. A single specimen without its air is a number nobody can defend a year later.

Is g/m² the same as momme?

No. Both express mass per unit area in different units, and converting between them is a separate question. The conversion is set out in the momme guide on this site; the sampling rules here apply to either unit.

Why is my own weighing lower than the supplier's report?

Usually because the air differs. The humidity step in Table 2 is larger than the hysteresis between the two directions, and both are large enough to be mistaken for a weight difference10. Ask for the conditioning state before treating the gap as a weight difference24.

Can a swatch result be used to accept a shipment?

Not on the strength of the number alone. ASTM D3776 concludes that results from its small-swatch option apply to the sample only and not necessarily to the lot2(https://dlvn.khoweb24h.com/uploads/files/ASTM%20D3776-09.pdf). A contract may accept one, but that is a term the parties wrote.

Which atmosphere should a silk test use?

ASTM D3776 asks for 21 ± 1 °C and 65 ± 2 % RH for textile weighing2. ISO 139 defines the standard atmosphere and a standard alternative usable by agreement3(https://www.iso.org/standard/35179.html). The specific figures are 20.0 °C and 65.0 % RH with tolerances of ±2.0 °C and ±4.0 % RH, transcribed from the standard listing rather than from its abstract12.

Does a heavier weight mean a thicker cloth?

No. ISO 5084 treats thickness as its own determination, under specified pressure11(https://www.iso.org/standard/23348.html). A mass per unit area figure says nothing about how thick the cloth is.
