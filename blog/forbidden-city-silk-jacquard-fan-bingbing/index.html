Mulberry Silk Jacquard and the Forbidden City Gown: Fan Bingbing's Met Gala

At the 2015 Met Gala, themed China: Through the Looking Glass, Fan Bingbing wore a gown with a green and gold cape. It opened from the back like a set of palace doors. The cloth came off the loom first: a mulberry silk jacquard with the palace motif in the weave. Embroidery was worked onto the finished gown afterwards in the Christopher Bu atelier. Red carpet coverage generally describes the colour and the cape, so the order of the two crafts is easy to miss.

Key takeaways

  • ›The gown starts as mulberry silk jacquard, with the palace motif woven on the loom; embroidery is added to the finished gown afterwards.
  • ›Jacquard puts the pattern inside the weave. Embroidery adds thread to a finished cloth. Both appear on this gown, at different stages.
  • ›The design draws on Forbidden City architecture: the central axis of the palace, the green and gold of its painted brackets.
  • ›Buyers specifying a jacquard should confirm the motif, the weave density, and the silk weight before the loom is set.

What the gown is made of

The gown is built around a cape that opens along the back, its green ground filled with gold lines and motifs drawn from the brackets and ceiling coffers of the Forbidden City in Beijing. The colors come from the palace itself: the green of its painted timber and the gold of its trim. Haiwainet described the dress as pure silk with colors drawn from the palace's bracket paintings, and noted that the look earned Fan Bingbing a place on American Vogue's best-dressed list for the night1. BeautiMode carried the same account of the gold-and-green gown and cape2. The motif is jacquard, formed by the loom as it raises and lowers individual warp threads, so those gold lines belong to the cloth. Embroidery was worked onto the finished gown afterwards, a sequence recorded in the atelier construction notes3.

Jacquard versus embroidery: the weave is the pattern

Jacquard is a weave. A jacquard loom controls each warp thread separately, so a motif can be built into the cloth as it is woven. Embroidery is the addition of thread to a finished cloth by hand or machine. Print applies ink to the surface. The three look different up close, and a buyer who asks for one should not be quoted another. Both stages are visible on this gown. The gold lines of the jacquard sit level with the green ground because the loom made them; embroidery worked on top afterwards stands proud of that surface. Reading which lines lie flat and which are raised tells a buyer whether the motif they are specifying belongs in the weave or in the stitch work.

How the pattern is made, by technique

How a jacquard motif is woven

A motif starts as a design, usually a drawing that is translated into a weave plan. The plan tells the loom which warp threads to lift and which to leave down for each row of weft. The raised and lowered threads form the pattern as the cloth grows. Because the motif is in the weave, it survives washing and wear the way a print or a surface embroidery cannot. For example, a gold line woven into a green ground stays level through repeated wear, where a stitched line would lift and catch. For a silk jacquard, the base yarn is mulberry silk, and the motif is woven in the same silk or a contrasting yarn. The density of the weave, the size of the motif repeat, and the weight of the silk are all set before the loom starts, which is why a jacquard order needs those three numbers up front.

The 2015 Met Gala moment

The 2015 Met Gala took its theme from the exhibition China: Through the Looking Glass. Fan Bingbing walked the red carpet in the Christopher Bu Forbidden City gown. The look was built on the palace's central-axis symmetry, so the cape opened from the back and spread to either side, the way the palace spreads around its central hall. The dress earned her a place on American Vogue's best-dressed list and drew coverage from the New York Times and Vogue, according to the Haiwainet account at the time1. The gown belongs to the same couture series as the Snow Plum Blossom dress Paz Vega wore to the 2018 Oscars. This gown came off the loom as a jacquard and was embroidered afterwards; the Snow Plum Blossom dress is the hand embroidery counterpart covered in its own case study.

Same series, two stages of work

The Christopher Bu "Forbidden City" couture series produced both dresses. Fan Bingbing's starts with a mulberry silk jacquard and is embroidered afterwards; Paz Vega's Snow Plum Blossom is mulberry silk with hand embroidery. The series was the designer's first haute couture line, and the atelier used both crafts across it. On this gown the woven motif comes first and the stitch work follows, so anyone describing the dress needs both stages in the right order. Red carpet coverage usually stops at the colour and the silhouette, which is why the sequence is easy to lose.

What to specify when you order a silk jacquard

A jacquard order needs three decisions before the loom is set. First, the motif and its repeat size, because the weave plan is built from it. Second, the weave density, which sets how firm or open the cloth is. Third, the silk weight and width, which fix the hand and the yield. A strike-off confirms the motif and density before bulk weaving starts, and it is the point where changes are cheap and later changes are not.
