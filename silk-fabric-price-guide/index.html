What silk fabric costs per meter, and what actually moves the price

Silk pricing is not a list; it is a function of six variables. Two suppliers can both quote “dupioni” at different prices and both be correct, because one is quoting 19 momme on finished cloth at 140 cm and the other is quoting 22 momme on greige at 114 cm with testing excluded. This page sets out the six drivers, the six ways a quotation misleads, and a twelve-point comparison that makes two quotes actually comparable.

Key takeaways

  • Six variables drive the number: grade, momme basis, construction, color route, quantity and width. Fix them and two quotes become comparable.
  • A momme figure measured on greige describes a heavier cloth than the same figure measured on finished fabric, so the basis belongs in the quotation.
  • Grade is measured on the yarn under GB/T 1797-2008, which means a price quoted on grade is describing raw material rather than the cloth you cut.
  • Delivery terms move costs rather than remove them: FOB leaves inland freight and insurance outside the number, and they arrive later as landed cost.
  • This page publishes no unit prices, because a single number would be accurate for one specification and misleading for every other.

Why this page contains no prices

A single headline number would be accurate for one specification and wrong for every other, and it would teach you to compare the wrong thing. Silk moves from greige utility cloth to custom-dyed, high-grade structured fabric by a multiple, not a percentage. So instead of a number, this page gives you the structure: fix the six variables, and any two quotations become directly comparable. We quote per order, and every quotation states its momme basis, grade, width and color route so it can be checked.

Three terms that decide what a quote means

Grade is the quality classification of the raw silk yarn. It is set under GB/T 1797-2008 (Standardization Administration of China, 2008), and it is measured on the yarn rather than on the cloth. Momme basis is the point in processing at which the weight was taken, greige or finished. It is the single field most often missing from a quotation. Landed cost is what the cloth costs once freight, insurance, duty and inland handling are added, which is why two identical FOB numbers can land at different totals under different delivery terms (International Chamber of Commerce, 2020).

For example, a quote at 19 momme finished and a quote at 19 momme greige are not the same cloth. The second finishes lighter, so the cheaper number is buying less silk. The same logic applies upward. The raw material floor comes from a reeling district that produces for export at scale (Yizhou District Government, 2023), and a price well below that floor is worth questioning rather than celebrating.

The six drivers

Each of these raises or lowers the price independently. Change two of them at once and the quotes stop being comparable, which is how most price confusion starts.

01

Fiber grade

Up with grade

4A, 5A and 6A are not marketing tiers. They are measured properties of the filament. Higher grade means fewer defects per unit length, which means more cocoons rejected at reeling and a higher cost per kilo of yarn. The price difference is real and it lands in the cloth: a 6A yarn dyes evenly on the first attempt, a lower grade often needs a second lot to fix banding.

02

Weight in momme

Up with momme

More silk per square meter is more fiber per meter of cloth. Moving from 16 to 22 momme is roughly a third more material in the same yard. This is the most predictable driver on the page, provided both quotations measure momme on the same side of degumming.

03

Construction complexity

Up with complexity

A plain-weave habotai runs off a loom at speed. A jacquard runs slower because every pick is selected, and pattern development adds a separate cost before a meter is woven. Structured cloths that need tight sett and heavy reed pressure also raise the loom time per meter.

04

Color route

Greige < card < custom

Four routes with different economics. Greige is the cheapest because nothing is dyed. A standard shade card amortises the dye lot setup over many orders. A custom match adds a lab dip plus a dedicated dye lot. Yarn-dyed or jacquard color introduces the color before weaving. A custom match on a small quantity is where quotations diverge most.

05

Quantity and width

Down with volume

Setup costs (loom dressing, dye lot, lab dip, inspection) are spread across the run. A narrow width at the same momme costs more per running meter because there is less cloth in the meter. Both effects are non-linear: the step from 300 m to 1,000 m usually matters more than 1,000 m to 3,000 m.

06

Testing and documentation

Up with scope

OEKO-TEX, GOTS, destination-market testing, inspection records and export documentation each carry a cost. They are also the first thing a quotation quietly drops when a price looks unusually low. If you need them, say so before quoting, not after.

Six ways a quotation misleads without lying

None of these are dishonest claims. Each is a true statement about a different thing, which is harder to catch.

Momme measured on greige, quoted as finished

The cloth is 22 momme before degumming and about 16–17 after. Both are true statements. Only one describes what you receive. This alone can explain a wide gap between two quotes for the same fabric name.

A lower grade sold under the same construction name

&ldquo;Dupioni&rdquo; describes a weave and a yarn source, not a grade. A 4A dupioni and a 6A dupioni are the same fabric name at different prices, and the difference shows up after dyeing, not on the swatch.

A narrower width

Quoting per meter without stating the width is the most common way to make a quote look competitive. 114 cm and 140 cm are not interchangeable when you are costing a marker: the narrower roll may need more meters for the same garment.

Standard shade presented as custom

Or the reverse: a custom match quoted at card price and then delivered as the nearest card shade. Ask which shade, and ask for the dip reference.

Testing excluded

A price that excludes OEKO-TEX or destination-market testing is not cheaper. It is a price for a different scope. Add the test cost back before comparing.

Different trade term

EXW, FCA and FOB prices are not comparable. The gap is the inland leg plus export clearance, and on silk it is not trivial. Always compare at the same Incoterm.

Where the saving comes from

When a quotation comes in well below others for a nominally identical fabric, the difference has to exist somewhere in the physical product. In practice it is one of four places: a lower grade, a lighter momme (often measured on greige), a narrower width, or reduced testing. Grade is the one worth checking first, because it is measured on the yarn against a published scale (Standardization Administration of China, 2008), and a downgrade is invisible in a swatch of the same weight and color.

None of those is automatically wrong: a lighter cloth at a narrower width may be exactly right for a lining program. The problem is only when the saving is invisible, because then it is discovered in the cutting room or after dyeing, when the cost of discovery is much higher than the saving.

Price questions buyers actually ask

How much does silk fabric cost per meter?

There is no single answer, and any page that gives you one number is describing one specification. Silk ranges from greige utility cloth at the low end to custom-dyed, high-grade, heavy structured fabric at the top: a multiple apart, not a percentage. The honest way to price a project is to fix six variables first: grade, momme measured on finished cloth, construction, color route, quantity and width. With those fixed, quotations from different suppliers become comparable. We quote per order for exactly this reason.

Why is silk fabric so expensive?

Because the raw material is labor-intensive to produce and the yield is low. A single cocoon yields one continuous filament of roughly 600 to 900 meters, and it takes several cocoons reeled together to make one 20/22 denier thread. On top of the fiber there is loom time, dyeing, finishing, inspection and the rejection rate at every stage. Weight compounds it: a 30 momme cloth uses nearly twice the silk of a 16 momme cloth of the same area.

Why do two suppliers quote very different prices for the same silk?

Almost always because one of the six variables is different and unstated: most often the momme basis (greige versus finished), the grade, the width, or whether testing is included. The fabric name is not a specification. Before assuming one supplier is cheaper, put both quotations side by side on the twelve points listed on this page; the gap usually explains itself.

Is Chinese silk cheaper than Italian silk?

They are different products in the same chain rather than two prices for one thing. China is the dominant origin for raw silk and greige; Italian mills are known for dyeing, finishing and quality grading, and much of the fiber they process is imported. A comparison only means something at the same specification and the same Incoterm. Otherwise you are comparing a raw material price to a finished luxury fabric price.

Does a bigger order always get a lower price?

Usually, but with diminishing returns. Setup costs (loom dressing, dye lot, lab dip, inspection) are spread over the run, so the first increase in quantity produces the largest drop per meter. Beyond a point the saving flattens, and on custom colors a larger run can even add risk, because you are committing to more meters of a shade that has only been approved as a lab dip.

How can I reduce cost without losing quality?

Change the specification deliberately rather than hoping the price falls. Move to a standard shade card color instead of a custom match. Take a slightly lighter momme where the garment allows it. Use a wider width so your marker wastes less. Consolidate several colors into one order to share the setup. For example, moving one lining style from a custom match to the nearest shade card color removes the lab dip and the dedicated dye lot from the quotation entirely. What does not work is asking for the same specification at a lower price: the difference has to come from somewhere, and it usually comes from grade, weight or testing.

Why do you not publish a price list?

Because a published number would be true for one combination of grade, weight, width, color and quantity and misleading for every other. Silk is quoted against a specification, and a price list would push buyers into comparing the wrong things. What we can do is quote quickly and state the basis clearly enough that our quotation can be checked against anyone else's.

Sources and method

Methodology: this page publishes no unit prices. Silk is quoted against a specification. A number without grade, momme basis, width, color route and quantity would be accurate for one combination and misleading for every other. The grade scale follows GB/T 1797-2008, which classifies the yarn rather than the cloth. Delivery terms follow the ICC's published Incoterms rules, because those decide which costs sit inside a quoted figure. The six drivers and the twelve comparison points come from quotations the atelier has reviewed, stated as structure rather than as a price list. Every figure that would depend on a specific mill or season is confirmed on the quotation instead.

  1. Standardization Administration of China: GB/T 1797-2008 Raw Silk (2008). The grade scale quoted in a raw silk price, which is measured on the yarn and not on the finished cloth.
  2. International Chamber of Commerce: Incoterms 2020 rules (2020). The delivery terms that decide which costs sit inside a quoted price and which arrive later as landed cost.
  3. Yizhou District Government: Yizhou district profile (2023). The reeling base behind the raw material that sets the floor of a fabric price.
